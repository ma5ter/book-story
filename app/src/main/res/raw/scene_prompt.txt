# ABSENT, UNSPECIFIED, AND INFERRED FEATURES

Distinguish explicitly between absence, inapplicability, and missing information.

- ABSENT: When the source, character catalog, or firmly established design indicates that a structure or feature is not present.

- UNSPECIFIED: When available information does not establish a feature's presence, absence, or detailed form.
- INFERRED: When a plausible detail is added to make the entity visually coherent, recognizable, or drawable. Prefer inferences supported by context, established morphology, general knowledge, or familiar character conventions.

Apply these distinctions consistently.

When features are UNSPECIFIED you should invent them instead of marking as "unspecified":
- Use reliable, entity-appropriate knowledge for recognizable real organisms, machines, and other familiar entities.
- Use established character designs, folklore, genre conventions, and contextual expectations for fictional or stylized entities when appropriate.

Do not replace unfamiliar construction with familiar human or animal anatomy merely for convenience. Be guided by the context, only if it implies human-like behavior then consider inventing human-alike features, e.g. singing implies mouth, looking - eyes.
- Prefer a small number of coherent, useful inferences over arbitrary detail.
- Once an inferred trait is established in the identity block, maintain that inference consistently throughout the scene.
- If the source explicitly establishes that an entity lacks a particular structure, preserve that absence. Never add the structure merely to improve expressiveness or visual familiarity.
- Equally, do not assume an entity is eyeless, faceless, limb-free, featureless, or incapable of a function merely because the source does not mention the corresponding structure.
- Do not invent biological organs for machines or mechanical parts for biological entities without supporting evidence or a clearly established hybrid construction.
- Do not equate visual resemblance with structural identity. A sensor is not automatically an eye, a gripper is not automatically a hand, and a housing is not automatically a head. Describe a familiar equivalent only when it is actually supported by the design.
